The Byzantine Empire, also referred to as the Eastern Roman Empire, was the continuation of the Roman Empire in its eastern provinces during Late Antiquity and the Middle Ages, when its capital city was Constantinople. It survived the fragmentation and fall of the Western Roman Empire in the 5th century and continued to exist for nearly a further millennium until the fall of Constantinople to the Ottoman Empire in 1453. The term Byzantine itself came from the original Greek name for the city of Constantinople, Byzantium, which was revived in the 1550s by the scholar Hieronymus Wolf as a term for the existing empire in the East, several decades after the empire had ceased to exist. The empire at its height under Emperor Justinian I in the 6th century controlled territory stretching from the Persian border in the east to the Spanish Mediterranean coast in the west, and from North Africa in the south to the Danube River in the north. Byzantine civilization, a term applied to the society and culture of the Byzantine Empire, was largely homogeneous, with Greek as the primary language though Latin was used in legal contexts and military texts. Byzantine art was highly distinctive, characterized by a preference for stylized, symbolic representations over the more naturalistic art of classical antiquity, with famous mosaics and icons adorning the interiors of churches throughout the empire. Byzantine architecture, best known for its churches with their domes and elaborate mosaics, drew on both Roman and Persian influences and created distinctive structures such as the Hagia Sophia, whose dome was a marvel of engineering that remained unsurpassed for nearly a thousand years. The Byzantine Empire played a crucial role in preserving classical Greek learning and literature, which would later be transmitted to Renaissance Italy through various channels including the conquests by the Ottoman Empire which drove Greek scholars westward. Byzantine theological disputes, particularly over icons and the nature of Christ, were central to the empire's internal conflicts and its relationships with both Western Europe and the Islamic world. Emperor Constantine IX Monomachos's reign saw the Byzantine Empire reach its greatest territorial extent since the 6th century, though this proved fleeting and subsequent emperors faced mounting pressures from Seljuk Turks in Asia Minor and Normans in the Balkans. The Battle of Manzikert in 1071 CE was a catastrophic defeat for the Byzantine Empire, resulting in the capture of Emperor Romanos IV Diogenes and opening Anatolia to Turkish settlement, a loss from which the empire never fully recovered. The First Crusade was prompted in part by Byzantine appeals for military assistance against the Seljuk Turks, though the subsequent relationship between the Crusader states and Constantinople was marked by rivalry and eventually led to the sack of Constantinople in 1204 during the Fourth Crusade. The Empire of Nicaea, established by Byzantine nobles in exile after the sack of Constantinople, managed to recapture Constantinople in 1261 and restore the empire, though it was never able to fully reclaim its former territories or power. Byzantine diplomacy was highly sophisticated, employing tactics including strategic marriages, treaties with carefully balanced obligations, and the calculated release of hostages to maintain peace. The Byzantine military employed a combination of heavily armored cavalry known as the katafraktion and light infantry armed with bows, adapting to threats from multiple directions including Persians, Arabs, Turks, Normans, and Slavic peoples. The empire's capital Constantinople was effectively impregnable to siege until the development of gunpowder artillery by the Ottomans in the 14th century, having successfully resisted numerous sieges over the centuries. Byzantine cuisine reflected the empire's position at the crossroads of Europe and Asia, incorporating ingredients and techniques from Persia, the Caucasus, and the Slavic lands. The fall of Constantinople in 1453 marked the end of the Byzantine Empire, though the Russian tsars claimed succession to the Byzantine imperial tradition through their marriage alliances with the final Byzantine princess. The legacy of Byzantium includes the preservation of Roman law through the Justinianic Code, which formed the basis for legal systems throughout Europe and the Middle East, as well as the Orthodox Church's distinctive character and its spread throughout the Slavic world. Byzantine manuscript traditions preserved countless works of classical Greek literature, philosophy, and science that would otherwise have been lost, including the complete surviving texts of Sappho and the majority of Plato's dialogues.
